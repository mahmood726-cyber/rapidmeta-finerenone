The question, and what is actually being estimated

In women at risk of HIV-1 acquisition, does a monthly vaginal ring releasing dapivirine reduce HIV-1 acquisition compared with a placebo ring?

The estimand this page reports is not the estimand the trials analysed, and that has to be said before any number. Both trials analysed HIV-1 acquisition as time to event, with censoring and unequal follow-up — ASPIRE reports 4,280 person-years and a median 1.6 years of follow-up. This page pools binary counts as a risk ratio. That is a different quantity. It is reported because it is what the underlying object supports, and it is labelled rather than presented as the trials' own answer.

The pooled hazard ratio is not computed here, and is refused rather than estimated. ASPIRE's hazard ratio is readable from its report; the Ring Study's confidence interval is not in any document held. Pooling one measured input with one assumed one would produce a number that looks like the trials' estimand and is not.

What that means in absolute terms

Across the two placebo arms, 153 of 1966 women acquired HIV-1 — 77.8 per 1000 over a median 1.6 years of follow-up. Applying the pooled risk ratio:

Risk per 1000 womenAbsolute reductionNumber needed to treat
Placebo77.8——
Dapivirine ring55.5 (44.5 to 69.2) 22.4 per 1000 (8.6 to 33.3)45 (30 to 116)

About 45 women use the ring for roughly 18 months to prevent one HIV-1 infection, and the plausible range runs from 30 to 116. The number needed to treat is given because a risk ratio alone does not tell a clinician or a programme what a ring costs per infection averted.

Interval methods, both reported and both named

With k = 2 the ordinary Wald interval above is the one carried. A Hartung–Knapp interval at k = 2 uses a t multiplier of 12.71, and Cochrane's guidance is that at this k it may be far too wide while the Wald interval is too narrow. Where a Hartung–Knapp interval is reported anywhere in this review it is the modified form, with the variance multiplier floored at max(1, Q/(k−1)) — without that floor an interval becomes narrower when studies agree more than chance, which is anti-conservative in exactly the situation small-k intervals exist to protect against.

Which counts were used, and what difference it makes

These are not two readings of one number. The registry posts counts as submitted. The publication reports them after endpoint adjudication — the step that decides which seroconversions count. Where the two disagree, the adjudicated figure is the trial's own final answer.

The Ring Study registry states 82/1302 versus 61/650; its adjudicated publication states 77/1300 versus 56/650. Five events and two participants differ in one arm.

The difference it makes: the pooled estimate moves from 0.7038 to 0.7127, and the interval from (0.5667–0.8742) to (0.5715–0.8889) — about one point on the effect and a slightly wider interval. It changes no clinical conclusion, and that is worth saying rather than hiding. The point is not that the correction was large; it is that a choice existed, was made, and is visible. A reader who prefers the registry figure can see exactly what they would get.

A disagreement with an external reviewer, shown rather than resolved silently

An external review supplied the pooled figure 0.7118 (0.5708–0.8877). This page computes 0.7127 (0.5715–0.8889). Both round to the published 0.71 (0.57–0.89).

The input that differs is one number: ASPIRE's placebo arm. The reviewer used 1313 in both arms. ASPIRE's results section reads: "Of 5516 women who underwent screening, 2629 were enrolled: 1313 in the dapivirine group and 1316 in the placebo group." The allocation was 1:1 but the realised split was not exactly balanced, and assuming balance is where the two calculations part.

This is what the audit trail is for. The disagreement is small and changes no conclusion — and it took thirty seconds to locate because the number carries the sentence it came from. A reader can check it without contacting anyone.

One of those two reasons — a withdrawn application — means the document does not exist; the other means our access failed. They are different and are written differently.

Breadth is a proxy; completeness is the property. This search returns two trials because two exist. A wider search would not add a trial; it would add non-trial literature. Any assessment of this review's search should be made against the eligible population, which is stated, rather than against the number of databases queried.

Relation to previously published syntheses

Published syntheses of these same two trials exist — including a Cochrane review and Musekiwa 2020. This review does not claim to be the first. An earlier version of this page stated that no published synthesis existed; that statement was wrong and is withdrawn.

The pooled estimate here, 0.7127 (0.5715–0.8889), reproduces the published 0.71 (0.57–0.89). Agreement with an independent synthesis is reported as corroboration, not as redundancy: two analyses reaching the same number from the same trials is evidence that neither contains an arithmetic error.

Population: what these trials support, and what they do not

The evidence does not support a claim about adolescent girls and young women. Enrolment extended to age 45 and the mean age was 26–27. Efficacy was not demonstrated in participants aged 18–21, and WHO's assessment records no demonstrated protection at ages 21 and below.

This review is therefore scoped to adult women. Where age-stratified estimates are wanted they must be extracted as such; a whole-population estimate must not be read as applying to the youngest stratum, in which it was not observed.

Follow-up: planned versus observed

ASPIRE's registered follow-up is 12–14 months. That is the planned figure. The observed follow-up was a median of 1.6 years, maximum 2.6 years, across 4,280 person-years. Earlier versions of this page displayed the planned duration in a field labelled as observed follow-up.

Age: where this works, and where it has not been shown to

The pooled estimate above is an average over a population aged 18 to 45 with a median age of 26. It does not apply uniformly, and the variation is large enough to change what should be offered to whom.

Age stratumEfficacy (95% CI)Reading
Over 2156% (31 to 71), P<0.001 Clear protection
18 to 2410% (−41 to 43)Not demonstrated
21 or younger−27% (−133 to 31), P = 0.45 No protection demonstrated; the point estimate favours placebo, on an interval far too wide to exclude either direction

These are post-hoc analyses and are labelled as such. They were not pre-specified, the strata were formed to give approximately equal numbers of infections, and a subgroup finding of this kind is hypothesis-generating. It is nonetheless the most decision-relevant result in these trials, it points the same way in both, and WHO records no demonstrated protection at ages 21 and below.

Adherence is the proposed explanation and it was measured, not assumed: the youngest stratum had both the lowest protection and the lowest adherence. That makes the age finding an adherence finding in disguise — which matters, because adherence is modifiable and age is not.

The ring does not protect against any other sexually transmitted infection. The gonorrhoea, chlamydia and trichomoniasis intervals all centre near 1. The syphilis interval spans 0.63 to 4.59 on a single trial and supports no conclusion in either direction — it should not be read as harm. A woman using this ring needs the same STI prevention she needed without it.

What a clinician or a programme should take from this

  • In women over 21 the ring works. 56% protection in the age-stratified analysis, and about 45 women using it for roughly 18 months prevents one infection.
  • In women 21 and under it has not been shown to work, and these trials cannot say whether that is the product or the adherence. Offering it as though the pooled figure applied would overstate what is known.
  • It is safe on everything measured — no excess of severe or serious adverse events, and no resistance signal among women who seroconverted.
  • It protects against nothing else. Condoms, STI screening and partner services remain necessary.
  • Effectiveness in use will be lower than this efficacy, which was already limited by adherence inside a trial with monthly contact.

What has happened since these trials were last synthesised

The most recent comparable synthesis searched literature to August 2020. On a live question, five years is not a neutral gap:

  • The European Medicines Agency extended its positive opinion to girls aged 16 and over — directly addressing the age stratum in which these two trials showed nothing.
  • WHO has recommended the ring as an additional prevention choice.
  • REACH and iMatter supply adolescent safety, adherence and acceptability evidence that did not exist when these trials were last synthesised.

None of that changes these two trials’ estimate, and none of it is pooled here. It changes who the estimate is being applied to — which is why a synthesis of these two trials alone is no longer sufficient to support a recommendation.

Limitations

  • One of two primary reports is not held. The Ring Study's counts are at secondary tier and its risk of bias is unassessed.
  • The pooled measure is a risk ratio over binary counts, while both trials analysed time to event. The pooled hazard ratio is refused, not estimated.
  • k = 2. Heterogeneity cannot be meaningfully estimated; I² of 0% at k = 2 carries almost no information.
  • No regulatory assessment was obtained, for two different reasons, one of which is that no FDA review exists.
  • ICTRP was not searched. For this question the eligible set is closed by registration, but that is an argument, not a search.
  • Adherence is reported but not modelled here, and it is the dominant effect modifier in both trials — the age finding is very likely an adherence finding.
  • The age-stratified results are post hoc, with strata chosen to equalise infection counts. They are the most decision-relevant results in these trials and the least methodologically secure, and both are true at once.
  • Four outcomes above are taken from a prior synthesis rather than read from the trial reports, because one primary report is not held. They are labelled at that tier.
