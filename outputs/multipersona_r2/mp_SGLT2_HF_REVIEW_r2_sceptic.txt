**Document A**

What I would attack:

1. The search is not a clean systematic search.
Already answered: yes. The document admits it directly: “No fresh systematic database search was run for this object, and nothing here should be read as though one had been.” It also admits the narrower ClinicalTrials.gov query “missed an included trial.”  
Defence: disclosure is strong, but it only defends honesty, not completeness. As a systematic review, this remains a real weakness.

2. No duplicate screening.
Already answered: yes. It says: “No second independent screen was run for this topic.”  
Defence: none, except confession. The page correctly calls this “owed.” That is not a methodological defence.

3. Post hoc eligibility and no prospective protocol.
Already answered: yes. It says: “Eligibility criteria were derived post hoc” and “No PROSPERO registration or protocol record is held on this object.”  
Defence: partially. It does not pretend otherwise, and says “a protocol specified before data collection is a historical fact about the past and cannot be created retrospectively.” That is good epistemic hygiene, but post hoc criteria still weaken the review.

4. Risk-of-bias handling looks internally awkward.
Already answered: partially. It says “9 result-level assessments were made,” but the GRADE table then says for both pooled outcomes: “No result-level RoB 2 assessment exists for this outcome.”  
Defence: weak. It downgrades because “unassessed is not low,” but the page leaves a nasty reader asking why the principal pooled results lack direct RoB 2 assessments.

5. The evidence base is tiny.
Already answered: yes. For k=3 it says tau-squared is estimated “from almost no information,” and for k=2 it says the same.  
Defence: good. It rates certainty LOW and does not oversell the precision.

6. Clinical usefulness is limited because there are no absolute effects or harms.
Already answered: yes. It says: “What this review does not give a clinician: the number of events in each arm, any absolute effect or number needed to treat and any measure of harm.”  
Defence: honest, but damaging. The document can support relative-effect comparison, not clinical decision-making.

7. The original four-trial composite was invalid.
Already answered: yes, and well. It says the four trials “do not share one endpoint” and that the k=4 pool “combined quantities that are not the same quantity.”  
Defence: strong. This is not a good attack anymore because the document withdraws the bad pool rather than hiding it.

8. The exclusion trail is not fully visible in the pasted page.
Already answered: only asserted. It says “Every screened trial appears with the quantity it reports quoted,” but the shown page gives aggregate counts, not the full row-level exclusion table.  
Defence: not really on the page as pasted. The claim may be true in the underlying object, but the visible document does not let me audit all 56 records.

Bottom line on A: the main attacks are real but mostly pre-empted. The serious remaining ones are no fresh full systematic search, no duplicate screen, post hoc methods, and imperfect RoB handling.

**Document B**

What I would attack:

1. It is under-described as a systematic review.
Already answered: barely. It says: “We searched PubMed and Embase for trials published up to Sept 24, 2018.”  
Defence: inadequate. No exact search string, no flow counts, no exclusions, no registry search, no risk-of-bias method, no certainty rating.

2. Search coverage is too narrow.
Already answered: yes, but only by admission of what was searched: “PubMed and Embase.”  
Defence: none on the page. Nothing says ClinicalTrials.gov, CENTRAL, regulatory sources, conference records, or unpublished trial results were searched.

3. The review is date-limited and now stale if read as current.
Already answered: yes. It states “published up to Sept 24, 2018.”  
Defence: acceptable only if the claim is historical. The page gives no update mechanism and no warning against current use.

4. Three trials are too few for confident subgroup claims.
Already answered: no. The page says it included “three identified trials,” then makes subgroup claims by ASCVD, heart failure history, and renal function.  
Defence: none on the page. The line “benefit only seen in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease” is too strong when the interaction p value is “0.0501.”

5. “Robust benefits” is overclaiming.
Already answered: no. The interpretation says SGLT2 inhibitors have “robust benefits” on heart failure hospitalisation and renal progression.  
Defence: none on the page. There is no GRADE, no RoB table, no heterogeneity statistic, and no sensitivity analysis shown in the provided text.

6. It pools a drug class without showing class-effect justification.
Already answered: no. It just says “SGLT2i reduced…” across trials.  
Defence: none. The document does not show whether effects differed by individual drug, trial design, or population.

7. Harms are absent.
Already answered: no. The methods say “Efficacy outcomes included…” and the findings report only benefits.  
Defence: none. A prevention review that omits safety on the page is incomplete.

8. Extraction/reviewer process is thin.
Already answered: partially. It says “Data search and extraction were completed with a standardised data form and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.”  
Defence: weak. That does not clearly say independent duplicate screening or extraction.

9. Conflicts are not dealt with.
Already answered: only funding. It says “Funding. None.”  
Defence: incomplete. Funding is not the same as author conflicts or trial sponsor influence.

Bottom line on B: the page gives headline results but not enough machinery to defend them. Most obvious attacks are not pre-empted.

**Which Is Better**

A is decisively better.

Not because A is flawless. It is not. It admits serious defects: “No fresh systematic database search,” “No second independent screen,” post hoc eligibility, LOW certainty, no absolute effects, no harms. But it puts those defects on the page and often refuses claims it cannot support.

B is much thinner. It gives attractive effect estimates and strong interpretation language, but the page does not defend the search, exclusions, bias assessment, certainty, subgroup reliability, class-effect assumption, or harms. The separator is auditability: A lets a hostile reviewer see the weak points; B mostly asks the reader to trust the abstract.
