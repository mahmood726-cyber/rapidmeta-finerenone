### DOCUMENT A
**IS ANYTHING WRONG?**
The document is highly misleading by omission because it exclusively reports relative risk reductions—claiming SGLT2i "reduced major adverse cardiovascular events by 11%", "reduced the risk of cardiovascular death or hospitalisation for heart failure by 23%", and "reduced the risk of progression of renal disease by 45%"—while entirely hiding the absolute event rates and absolute risk reductions. For instance, the authors report only "766 renal composite outcomes" out of "34 322 patients," which is a raw event rate of barely 2.2%. Presenting a "45%" relative reduction without this absolute context tricks the reader into believing the clinical benefit is huge, when it is actually minimal for most of this primary prevention population. 

Additionally, the document completely omits any mention of adverse events (such as ketoacidosis, genital mycotic infections, or amputations). A reader would incorrectly conclude that these drugs have absolutely no harms.

The conclusion is also overstated relative to the evidence when it claims "robust benefits on reducing hospitalisation for heart failure and progression of renal disease regardless of existing atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or a history of heart failure." These were diabetes cardiovascular outcome trials, not dedicated heart failure trials. The subset of patients with a baseline "history of heart failure" was small (around 10-15%), poorly defined by unadjudicated investigator checkboxes, and lacked baseline ejection fraction measurements. Claiming "robust benefits" in established heart failure from this cohort alone drastically overstates what this specific dataset can prove.

### DOCUMENT B
**IS ANYTHING WRONG?**
The document actively excludes valid, peer-reviewed data from the DELIVER trial from its primary two-component pool, which is factually misleading and ruins the integrity of the meta-analysis. It justifies this by stating: "DELIVER (NCT03619213) does not post the first-event two-component outcome... Its harmonised value exists in the publication, not the registration. A k=3 pool we can fully vouch for beats A k=4 with one input we cannot". 

This is extreme methodological pedantry that severely distorts the evidence base. DELIVER is a foundational HFpEF trial; by intentionally ignoring its published data simply because it was not in the registry in the desired format, the review fails to reflect the true evidence "across the ejection fraction spectrum" promised in its title. A reader would wrongly conclude that the harmonized endpoint could only be assessed in 3 trials.

Furthermore, it intentionally omits the basic data required for clinical decision-making. It plainly states: "What this review does not give a clinician: the number of events in each arm, any absolute effect or number needed to treat and any measure of harm." Presenting a medical review that completely strips away absolute effects and all measures of harm leaves the reader with a clinically useless document, as they have no way to weigh risk against benefit.

### WHICH IS BETTER, A OR B?
Document A is decisively better. 

What separates them is that Document A actually synthesizes the included trials to deliver a clinical conclusion, whereas Document B sacrifices the totality of the evidence for methodological rigidity. Document A commits the typical sins of a medical abstract—hyping relative effects and hiding safety data—but it leverages all of its included data. Document B intentionally discards a quarter of the most critical evidence in heart failure (the DELIVER trial) simply because the data was found in the "publication, not the registration." A review that willfully ignores published landmark trial results to satisfy an arbitrary rule about registries fails at the fundamental purpose of evidence synthesis. Document B's outright refusal to provide "any absolute effect or number needed to treat and any measure of harm" further cements its uselessness compared to Document A.
