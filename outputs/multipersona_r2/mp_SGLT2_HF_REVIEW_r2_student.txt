**Document A**

Yes, I could improve it without being completely misled, because it repeatedly tells me what not to claim. But it is messy enough that I could still make serious mistakes.

What I would wrongly believe if I skimmed it: that there is a clean four-trial pooled answer “across the ejection fraction spectrum.” The title says “the four randomised outcome trials,” and the introduction says, “It pools 4 randomised trials,” but the results later say: “These 4 trials are not pooled.” That is a trap. I would need to rewrite this so the main message is not accidentally “four trials pooled.”

The worst misleading area is risk of bias. It says: “Risk of bias was assessed with RoB 2 at the level of each reported result. Across the 9 results assessed: 9 at some concerns.” But the certainty table then says: “No result-level RoB 2 assessment exists for this outcome, so risk of bias is not assessed rather than absent.” I would not write “RoB 2 showed some concerns for the pooled outcomes” unless I had the actual result-level judgements in front of me.

I could check the NCT numbers, endpoint definitions, trial eligibility, posted HRs, and whether DAPA-HF/DELIVER used a three-component endpoint while EMPEROR used two components. I could also recalculate the pooled HRs from log HRs and CIs if the extraction table were available. I would have to take on trust the hidden/collapsed source trail, the full screening log, the RoB 2 domain judgements, and the claim that the “unscreened remainder is 0.”

The document mostly protects me from overclaiming. It explicitly says: “No fresh systematic database search was run,” “No second independent screen was run,” and “What this review does not give a clinician: the number of events in each arm, any absolute effect or number needed to treat and any measure of harm.” That is good. But I could still mistakenly turn “hazard ratio 0.764” into a treatment recommendation. The document itself says not to.

**Document B**

I would be much more likely to be misled by this one. It is smooth, confident, and under-documented.

The most dangerous sentence is: “with benefit only seen in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.” That is too strong for the quoted interaction result: “p for interaction=0.0501.” A medical student could easily rewrite that as a real subgroup effect, when it is borderline at best and not enough, by itself, to say benefit is “only” in one group.

Another overconfident sentence is: “they have robust benefits on reducing hospitalisation for heart failure and progression of renal disease regardless of existing atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or a history of heart failure.” The document does not give the subgroup HRs, CIs, heterogeneity, risk of bias, or event counts by subgroup. I would have to trust the authors’ summary.

What I could check myself: identify the three trials, verify the 34,322 total patients, extract the HRs, check whether the renal outcome definitions match, and see whether the PubMed/Embase search could really find all eligible trials up to “Sept 24, 2018.” But the abstract gives no search string, no trial names, no flow diagram, no risk-of-bias assessment, no pooling model, and no heterogeneity statistics. Most of the machinery is invisible.

The title also invites overreach: “primary and secondary prevention” sounds broader than “patients with type 2 diabetes” in “cardiovascular outcome trials.” I would not generalise this to patients without diabetes, to modern heart-failure trials, or to absolute clinical benefit. It gives relative HRs, not absolute risks or NNTs.

**Which Is Better**

A is decisively better for this task. It is uglier and internally confusing in places, but it exposes missing data, withdrawn analyses, post-hoc eligibility, no duplicate screening, no protocol, and no absolute effects.

B is cleaner prose, but that is exactly the danger. It lets strong subgroup claims survive without showing enough of the checks. The separator is transparency: A gives me reasons to doubt it; B mostly asks me to trust it.
