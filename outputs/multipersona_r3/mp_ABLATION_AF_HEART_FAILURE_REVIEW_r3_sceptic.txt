=== DOCUMENT A ===

WHAT WOULD YOU ATTACK, and CAN IT BE DEFENDED FROM WHAT IS ON THE PAGE?

1. Reporting a heterogeneity statistic for unpooled trials. 
Attack: The abstract claims "The trials agreed closely (I-squared 0%)", but the Results section explicitly states, "These 2 trials are not pooled". It is contradictory to report an I-squared for an outcome the document refuses to meta-analyse.
Defended? No. The document does not explain or defend this contradiction.

2. The lack of a bibliographic search.
Attack: The review did not search any standard bibliographic databases.
Defended? Yes, this is explicitly pre-empted. The document openly admits: "No bibliographic search for primary trials was run. The 2 included trials were identified by reading named registrations".

3. The refusal to pool data.
Attack: The review fails to provide a synthesized pooled estimate for the trials.
Defended? Yes. The document defends this explicitly: "The two trials define the heart-failure limb of their composite differently... Two composites of the same shape are not the same quantity, and pooling them would report an average over two definitions as though it were one effect."

4. Unregistered protocol and post hoc criteria.
Attack: The eligibility criteria were created after the fact and the review is unregistered.
Defended? Yes. The document defends the criteria by citing authority: "Eligibility criteria were derived post hoc... Authority: Mecir R107 permits post-hoc eligibility criteria provided they are declared as such." It also pre-empts the lack of registration: "No PROSPERO registration or protocol record is held on this object."

5. Clinical uselessness.
Attack: The review provides no absolute effects, making it useless for clinical decision-making.
Defended? Yes, pre-empted explicitly: "What this review does not give a clinician: the number of events in each arm, any absolute effect or number needed to treat and any measure of harm... this review can support a judgement about what these trials found and not a judgement about what to do."

6. Lack of duplicate screening.
Attack: The review failed to use independent duplicate screening.
Defended? Yes, pre-empted explicitly: "No second independent screen was run for this topic | This is recorded as owed rather than described".

=== DOCUMENT B ===

WHAT WOULD YOU ATTACK, and CAN IT BE DEFENDED FROM WHAT IS ON THE PAGE?

1. Inadequate search strategy risking publication bias.
Attack: The search is highly limited. The text states "We searched PubMed and Embase", completely omitting trial registries or other major databases.
Defended? No. There is no defence provided for this limited search strategy.

2. Absence of risk of bias and certainty of evidence assessment.

Defended? No. The text does not mention risk of bias, trial quality, or GRADE.

3. Unregistered protocol.
Attack: There is no mention of a prespecified protocol or PROSPERO registration.
Defended? No. The document does not mention registration or defend its absence.

4. Unjustified subgroup analyses.
Attack: The document reports multiple subgroup interaction tests ("p for interaction=0.0501", "p for interaction=0.0073", "p for interaction=0.0258") without stating whether these subgroups were prespecified or the result of post hoc data-dredging.
Defended? No. The text does not state whether subgroups were prespecified.

5. Unclear data extraction methodology.
Attack: The document states "Data search and extraction were completed with a standardised data form and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus", but fails to specify if extraction was performed independently and in duplicate.
Defended? No. The document does not clarify the extraction process beyond this vague statement.

=== WHICH IS BETTER ===

Document A is decisively better. 

What separates them is methodological transparency and radical honesty about limitations. Document A relentlessly pre-empts attacks by explicitly documenting its own flaws: it admits "No bibliographic search for primary trials was run", confesses "No second independent screen was run", clearly flags that criteria were "derived post hoc", and bluntly warns a clinician that it lacks "any absolute effect or number needed to treat". Document B, conversely, presents itself as a standard, completed systematic review while silently hiding catastrophic methodological omissions: it does not assess risk of bias, does not mention a protocol, relies on an inadequate search ("We searched PubMed and Embase"), and reports multiple subgroup p-values without stating if they were prespecified. Document A is structurally honest about its own limits; Document B hides its failures behind conventional academic prose.
