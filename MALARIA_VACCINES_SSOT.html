What matches

    The review reports a number of included studies equal to the number of distinct randomised COHORTS this object located independently.   • Direction and rough magnitude for R21
    The review describes this vaccine as showing high efficacy, which agrees with this object's pooled seasonal result.
  • Waning
    The review reports that protective efficacy declined over time, and this object does not contradict it. An earlier version of this entry went further and said this object's own rows demonstrate the same pattern, because the longest follow-up carried the smallest estimate. That was withdrawn on review: it is not true of these rows. The longest follow-up row is neither the smallest efficacy here -- the infant cohort at a shorter follow-up is smaller -- nor the smallest ratio. These rows differ from one another in vaccine, estimand, age and window all at once, so the variation between them cannot be attributed to follow-up length, and a cross-sectional read of them is not evidence of waning. Waning is a within-cohort question, and the trials that answer it do so inside their own reports rather than in this comparison.

What this object corrects

  • A single pooled risk ratio for BOTH vaccines together
    One risk ratio presented as covering both vaccines in the older age group.
    Refused as a primary result. The two vaccines' pivotal trials do not report the same quantity: one reports a hazard ratio for the time to a first episode over a year, the other a negative-binomial rate ratio for all episodes over its whole follow-up. A single ratio across both is a weighted average of two different estimands. This object reports each vaccine separately and carries NO combined figure at all. An earlier version of this sentence said the combined figure was confined to an outcome declared exploratory; that outcome no longer carries a pooled point, an interval or an efficacy percentage, because the gate removed them, so the sentence had gone stale against the object it describes.
  • Ordinary binary risk ratios for time-to-event and rate endpoints
    Risk ratios, the effect measure of a table of participants with and without an event.
    Both pivotal trials analysed something else. One used Cox regression on the time to a first episode; the other used negative binomial regression on all episodes with person-time as the offset, so its published counts are EPISODES and exceed the number of participants. Treating either as a table of participants changes the estimand and, for the episode counts, uses a numerator that cannot sit over a participant denominator at all. This object stores every effect on the log scale with its standard error derived from the published interval, and its validator blocks a survival or rate endpoint stored as a binary one.
  • An event rate reported as though it were a vaccine efficacy
    An event rate quoted for R21 alongside the efficacy discussion, in the words the staged record gives and no more.
    Not carried in any form. The staged abstract and repository record call it a low event rate and do not say what it is a rate OF, over what denominator, or in which arm, so there is not enough there to treat it as a contrast and it is not used as one. An earlier version of this entry asserted it was a single-arm proportion with no comparator in it. That may well be right, but the staged record does not say so, and a correction that over-reads its source is the fault it is correcting.

What could not be resolved

  • Possible unit-of-analysis double counting
    Both pivotal programmes publish more than one contrast against a SHARED control group, and both were published more than once at different follow-up points. A review that counts publications rather than randomised cohorts, or that uses more than one contrast per shared control, would double-count control participants. Whether this review did either cannot be determined from the material that was retrievable. This object makes the corresponding choice explicit on every row: contrasts sharing a control carry the same control key, one is chosen, and the rest are carried outside every pool.
  • Which R21 figure entered an age-banded analysis
    The review reports analysing an age band matching the older RTS,S cohort. The R21 phase 3 enrolled a WIDER band extending beyond it, and found efficacy to differ significantly by age within its own population, so the trial's overall figure and its figure for the narrower band are not the same number. Which of the two a reader of the review is seeing therefore matters. An earlier draft of this reconciliation asserted that the review had used the wider trial's overall figure; that assertion is WITHDRAWN, because the review also publishes the narrower band as its analysis population and the trial publishes a subgroup for exactly that band, so the review could legitimately have extracted the subgroup. Without the included-study table there is no basis to say which it did, and saying so anyway would be the same kind of over-claim this object criticises elsewhere. What is recorded instead is the fact that makes the question live, and this object's own response to it: every row here states its own population rather than being aligned to a common band.

The methods authority these decisions rest on

Higgins JPT, Thomas J, Chandler J, Cumpston M, Li T, Page MJ, et al (editors). Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions version 6.5.1. Cochrane, 2025.
https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook

Where this object had to decide whether something could be combined, the decision was referred to the Handbook rather than to a rule invented here. The sections relied on are listed with what each one settled, so a reader can check the reasoning against the source rather than against this object's own assertion of it.

SectionTitleWhat it settled here
3.2.3Defining which comparisons will be madeDistinguishes intervention-versus-placebo and intervention-versus-control from intervention A versus intervention B. The chemoprevention trial is the third kind, which is why its ratio is never reported as an efficacy.
4.6.1Studies (not reports) as the unit of interestReviews have STUDIES as the unit of analysis and a single study may have several reports. This is the authority for counting cohorts rather than publications.
4.6.2Identifying multiple reports from the same studyAssociating the several reports of one cohort with each other. The cohorts this was done for are listed on the deduplication decision itself; no count is restated here, because the count restated here was wrong twice.
23.3.2Determining which intervention groups are relevantThe governing rule where a trial randomised more than two groups: which of them the review's own question concerns. Each pooled outcome names one regimen, and that is what selects the arm.
23.3.4How to include multiple groups from one studyIts prohibition on entering the same comparator group more than once, which it calls a unit-of-analysis error -- the reason a choice is compulsory here. Its four options, and its caveat against arbitrary pair-selection, are both recorded on the decision rather than omitted.
10.8Meta-analysis of counts and ratesEstablishes the RATE RATIO as the effect measure for count and rate data, combined as log rate ratios by the generic inverse-variance method. This is the measure and the method used for the RTS,S pooled result.
10.9Meta-analysis of time-to-event outcomesEstablishes the HAZARD RATIO as the effect measure for time-to-event data, combined as log hazard ratios by the same generic method. This is the measure and method used for the R21 pooled result -- and it is a DIFFERENT measure from the one in section 10.8, which is why the two vaccines' results are not combined with each other.
10.6Combining dichotomous and continuous outcomesRead as the limiting case rather than as permission. Where the Handbook sanctions combining unlike measures it names the conversion; it provides one between odds ratios and standardised mean differences, and none between a hazard ratio and a rate ratio.
10.10.1What is heterogeneity?The distinction between clinical and methodological diversity on one hand and statistical heterogeneity on the other. Differences in follow-up length and in the fever criterion are documented as diversity rather than treated as disqualifying.
10.10.2Identifying and measuring heterogeneityThe I-squared bands. Both pooled results here fall in the lowest band; the cross-vaccine combination that was removed fell in the highest.
10.10.3Strategies for addressing heterogeneityIts strategy 'do not do a meta-analysis', on the stated grounds that where results vary considerably an average may mislead. This is the authority for removing the cross-vaccine pooled figure. An earlier version called it the section's FIRST-listed strategy; it is not. The section opens with 'Check again that the data are correct', and a review leg was right about the order. The chapter was reopened to confirm it before the claim was withdrawn, because that leg has been wrong about section content before and a track record is not evidence either way.
10.11Investigating heterogeneitySubgroup analysis as what REPLACES a combined estimate that should not be computed. Its subsection on whether the effect differs between subgroups says a FORMAL statistical approach SHOULD be used where subgroups are compared, and warns against comparing within-subgroup inferences such as P values. It does NOT ask that subgroups be left untested, and an earlier version of this entry said it did -- a reviewer called that a misrepresentation and was right. The strata here are reported and not compared for a reason that section supplies no remedy for: they carry DIFFERENT effect measures, a hazard ratio against a rate ratio, and the test it describes is a heterogeneity test across subgroup results, which presumes one measure. The comparison is declined because it cannot be made validly, NOT because the Handbook asks for it to be declined. This section is also NOT the authority for dropping the combined figure -- that is 10.10.3, and the two are easy to confuse.
10.2Introduction to meta-analysisWhat a meta-analysis IS -- the combination of results across studies -- and therefore why a row that only one cohort reports over its window is presented alone. It is cited where a measure-specific section would once have been: an earlier version cited the rate-ratio section beside a row that expressly declines to call its measure a rate ratio, and a reviewer was right that a section cannot govern a measure the row refuses to claim.
10.10.4Incorporating heterogeneity into random-effects modelsThe choice of a random-effects model AND of the estimator for the between-study variance. The effect-measure sections do not govern either, which a reviewer pointed out. Its subsections carry the record that other estimators perform better than DerSimonian and Laird, which is why the headline here is restricted maximum likelihood, and the advice to compare the methods when there are only two or three studies. An earlier version of this sentence said 'better than the one kept here', which was true when DerSimonian and Laird was the headline and became false the moment it stopped being; a reviewer caught the remnant.
10.14Sensitivity analysesThe requirement that a pooled result be shown robust to influential decisions. Both pooled outcomes carry a leave-one-out analysis testing the decision that was challenged in review.
